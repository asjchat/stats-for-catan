Preface

Why I Wrote This

This project started when I enrolled in my first probability theory course and realized that Catan was a direct application of nearly everything I was studying. Expected value, variance, the law of large numbers, conditional probability — concepts I was encountering as abstract formulas had concrete, immediate relevance every time two dice hit the table. This text is an attempt to make that connection explicit.

You do not need a statistics background but you do need to know the rules of Catan. Part I covers the pure mathematics of the dice and the expected-value reasoning that should underlie every decision. Subsequent parts extend this framework to settlement placement, resource economics, the robber, multiplayer game theory, and positional strategy.

Part I

The Engine of the Game

Pure mathematics of dice, probability, and the expected-value framework that underpins every decision.

Chapter 1

The Dice Control Everything

Every resource in Catan originates from a single mechanical event: two six-sided dice are rolled, their values summed, and hexagonal terrain tiles whose number tokens match that sum produce one resource card for each adjacent settlement (and two for each adjacent city). This seems simple. It is, in fact, a deep probabilistic structure that governs the entire game, and understanding it precisely is the first task of the mathematical Catan player.

The Sample Space of Two Dice

When you roll one fair six-sided die, the sample space contains 6 equally likely outcomes: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. When you roll two dice, the combined sample space contains 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely ordered pairs, from (1,1) to (6,6). The sum of two dice ranges from 2 to 12, but crucially, the sums are not equally likely. The number of ways to achieve each sum determines its probability.

Figure 1.1 — All possible sums from two six-sided dice, combination counts, and probabilities. The distribution is symmetric around 7, which occurs with probability 6/36 = 16.67%.

The distribution is a discrete triangle, symmetric around 7. Seven is the single most probable outcome at 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 16.67%. This matters for two distinct reasons: 7 triggers the robber rather than producing resources, and the numbers 6 and 8 (5/36 each) become the most valuable production numbers in the game.

The triangular distribution of two-dice sums is sometimes compared to a normal (bell) curve, but this analogy should not be taken literally — the sum of two dice produces a discrete triangular distribution, not a normal one. The comparison is only a rough visual similarity. In a typical game of 60–70 rolls, you can expect the following approximate counts for key numbers:

Variance and the Law of Large Numbers

The law of large numbers states that as the number of trials increases, the observed average converges to the expected value. In practical terms: your 6 will eventually produce at its theoretical rate, but in any given game of 65 rolls, it might fire only 2 times or as many as 11 times — both are within normal variance.

The standard deviation for the sum of two dice is approximately 2.42. As a rough approximation, 68% of rolls fall within 2.42 of the mean (7), which spans the range 4.58–9.42, corresponding to the integer sums 5 through 9. Note this is an approximation: the true probability P(5 ≤ sum ≤ 9) = 24/36 ≈ 66.7%, slightly under 68% because the distribution is discrete and triangular rather than truly normal.

The Robber: Seven as a Special Case

Seven is not just the most probable sum; it is mechanically distinct. When a 7 is rolled, no resources are produced, the active player moves the robber to any hex (blocking that tile and stealing a card from an adjacent opponent), and any player holding eight or more cards must discard half. This triple punishment makes 7 the single most impactful number in the game.

In a 65-roll game, expect approximately 10–11 robber activations — roughly one in six turns. A resource-rich settlement sitting next to a frequently targeted hex will lose more than its dot count suggests. We quantify this in Part IV.

Chapter 2

Randomness and the Expected Value Framework

The previous chapter established what the dice can produce. This chapter establishes how to think about those dice — specifically, how to use expected value to evaluate choices under uncertainty. Expected value is the single most powerful tool in a Catan player’s analytical toolkit.

Defining Expected Value

The expected value (EV) of a random variable is its probability-weighted average outcome. For a discrete random variable X with outcomes x₁, x₂, …, xn and associated probabilities p₁, p₂, …, pn:

E[X] = Σ (xᵢ × pᵢ) = x₁p₁ + x₂p₂ + … + xnpn

Expected value does not tell you what will happen in any single trial. It tells you the average outcome across many trials. The EV of rolling a single die is 3.5, even though 3.5 can never appear on the die.

Expected Resources Per Turn

For a settlement adjacent to a single hex bearing number token N, the expected resources produced per turn equals the probability of rolling N. In the base case (no robber present), the expected resources per turn from one hex equals that probability directly:

The Concept of Pips

Experienced Catan players use the term “pips” (the dots on number tokens) as a shorthand for production value. Since the number of dots equals the probability numerator over 36, total pips for a settlement equals the numerator of its total production probability. A settlement with 12 total pips has an expected production of 12/36 = 1/3 of a resource per turn.

The theoretical arithmetic maximum pip count for a single settlement is 15 (three high-value hexes sharing a vertex, e.g., 6–8–6). However, this is unreachable in standard Catan play: the official setup rules prohibit placing the 6 and 8 tokens on adjacent hexes, and two tokens of the same number cannot be adjacent either. In practice, the highest legally achievable pip count is 13 (e.g., 6–8–5 or 6–8–9). Competitive pip counts cluster between 10 and 13.

Expected Value Across a Full Game

Assume an average of 65 total rolls with 10–11 sevens, leaving approximately 54–55 productive rolls. A settlement with 12 pips has an EV of 12/36 = 0.333 resources per productive turn. Over 54 productive turns, the expected total production is 0.333 × 54 = 18 resources. A city on the same spot doubles this to approximately 36 resources.

EV and Decision-Making

The practical application of EV is not to compute precise decimals at the table. It is to build a consistent framework for comparing options. When evaluating any decision — where to place a road, whether to trade, when to build versus save — the EV framework asks: given the range of possible outcomes and their probabilities, which choice produces the highest expected payoff?

This framework does not guarantee winning. A high-EV decision can yield a poor outcome; that is the nature of randomness. But over the course of an entire game, consistently choosing positive-EV actions will outperform decisions made on instinct or emotion.

Chapter 3

Long-Run vs. Short-Run Thinking

The tension between long-run expected value and short-run variance is the defining psychological challenge of Catan. Players who conflate the two make systematic strategic errors — either over-weighting a bad run of luck (“my 6 never rolls”) or under-weighting the real impact of unlucky streaks on a game that lasts only 60–70 turns.

The Game as a Statistical Sample

A typical Catan game involves 60–75 rolls of the dice — a relatively small sample size from a statistical perspective. For comparison, a casino roulette wheel must spin thousands of times before observed frequencies closely mirror theoretical probabilities. With 65 dice rolls, noticeable deviation from expected values is not a flaw in your analysis; it is a mathematical certainty.

The coefficient of variation (CV) for dice-roll frequency is especially high for low-probability numbers. For a number with probability 1/36 (like 2 or 12), the expected count in 65 rolls is 1.8 with a standard deviation of approximately 1.32 — a CV of 73%. Using the binomial distribution, seeing zero rolls of that number has probability ~13%, and seeing three or more has probability ~26%. In short, wide swings from the expected count of ~2 are common and should be anticipated.

Standard Deviation of Roll Counts

For a number that appears with probability p in n total rolls, the expected count is np, and since each roll is a Bernoulli trial, the standard deviation is √(np(1−p)).

The practical takeaway: your 6-token hex is expected to produce 9 times but could reasonably produce anywhere from 6 to 12 times. Your 2-token hex may well produce zero times in a given game. Planning for only expected value without acknowledging variance leads to fragile strategies.

Diversification as Variance Reduction

Spreading your production across multiple numbers reduces the impact of any individual number’s variance. If your entire economy depends on a single 8-token hex, you are fully exposed to that number’s variance. A bad run — say, 4 rolls instead of 9 — could be catastrophic. If instead you have production on a 6, an 8, and a 5, poor performance on one is partially offset by normal or above-average performance on the others.

This is the same principle that underlies portfolio diversification in finance. The dice rolls in Catan are independent events, making number diversification a mathematically sound risk-reduction strategy.

When to Trust Your EV Analysis

EV calculations become increasingly reliable as the number of productive rolls you stand to benefit from increases. In the first 20 rolls, a 6-token hex could easily under-perform by 3–4 rolls with no recourse. By roll 50, the law of large numbers has had more opportunity to operate, and extreme deviations become less likely.

This suggests a timing implication: in the early game, a highly variable but high-EV position may be preferable to a safe, moderate-EV position, because there is more game ahead for the EV to materialize. In the late game, when only 10–15 rolls remain, a position with lower but more reliable production — or one that does not depend on production at all (port arbitrage, development cards) — may be strategically superior.

Part II

Settlement Mathematics

From raw probability to placement decisions: how to measure, compare, and optimize where you build.

Chapter 4

Calculating the Strength of a Settlement

Part I established that every Catan resource originates from a dice roll and that different number tokens carry different probabilities. Part II applies that framework to the concrete question a player faces before every new settlement: which vertex on this board is actually worth building on? This chapter develops a rigorous metric for answering that question and works through comparisons on real board configurations.

The Production Score

The most direct measure of a settlement's value is its production score: the sum of pip values across all adjacent terrain hexes, excluding the desert. Because each pip equals 1/36 probability of producing one resource per turn, the production score is exactly 36 times the settlement's expected output per turn.

Why Resource Type Still Matters

Production scores treat all resources as equivalent. In isolation that is fine; in context it is incomplete. A settlement producing 12 pips of ore alone is not as flexible as one producing 10 pips spread across wood, brick, and grain, because the mono-resource player must trade for everything else. The implicit cost of each trade is between one and three extra resources, depending on port access. A raw pip comparison ignores that tax entirely.

The practical correction is to track resource diversity alongside production score and to penalize, mentally, any location that collapses onto a single resource type you already produce abundantly. This adjustment is qualitative but systematic: before committing to a high-pip location, ask whether the resources it provides are ones you genuinely need or ones you will be forced to give away at a loss.

The Intersection Quality Rating

To fold both production and diversity into a single comparable number, we define the Intersection Quality Rating (IQR):

IQR = Production Score + (2 x number of distinct resource types adjacent)

The +2 per resource type is a heuristic that approximates the trading efficiency gained by self-supplying a resource rather than acquiring it at a 3:1 or 4:1 rate. Locations with an IQR above 18 are prime; below 12, they are generally not worth a road investment to reach.

Example 4.1 -- Two Apparently Equal Locations Location A: adjacent to 8 (ore), 6 (grain), 3 (brick). Pips: 5 + 5 + 2 = 12. Three resource types. IQR = 12 + 6 = 18.

Location B: adjacent to 9 (wheat), 9 (ore), 5 (sheep). Pips: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. Three resource types. IQR = 12 + 6 = 18.

The IQR scores are identical, confirming that both locations offer comparable expected value. The difference lies in variance: Location A has a high-production anchor (two 5-pip tokens), meaning it will produce more reliably on any given turn. Location B spreads its production more evenly across three mid-range numbers, which is slightly safer against individual number droughts but produces in smaller, more predictable increments. A risk-averse player marginally prefers A; a player chasing production consistency may prefer B.

Port Adjacency

A settlement vertex that also touches a port provides an ongoing trading benefit worth a meaningful IQR premium. As a working rule: add 3 IQR points for a 2:1 port that matches a resource you produce in volume, and 1 point for a 3:1 generic port. Port valuation is covered in full in Chapter 9; the point here is that port adjacency is a genuine, measurable component of location quality and should not be treated as a secondary concern.

Chapter 5

Production and Balance: Resource Diversity vs. Output

Chapter 4 introduced the IQR as a composite measure of location quality. This chapter goes deeper: when is raw output worth sacrificing for broader resource coverage, and when does concentration pay off? The answer turns on port access, your opponents' positions, and where in the game you find yourself.

The Cost of a Missing Resource

If your settlements produce no wood, every road and every settlement you build requires acquiring wood through trade. At a 3:1 generic port, you pay 3 of any resource for 1 wood. At the bank's default rate, 4 for 1. Each road (which needs 1 wood + 1 brick) then costs 4 or 5 total resources instead of 2. The missing resource premium per road is 2 to 3 additional resources.

Over a full game, a player building four roads and three settlements while missing wood might pay an extra 12 to 15 resources in trade inefficiency. At a production rate of roughly 0.3 resources per productive turn, recovering that deficit requires approximately 40 to 50 extra turns of production -- more turns than a typical game contains. The implication is stark: a structural resource gap, left unaddressed, is not a temporary inconvenience but a persistent economic penalty.

Measuring Balance: The Concentration Index

The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), a standard tool from industrial economics, provides a precise measure of resource concentration. For a player's pip distribution across all five resource types:

HHI = sum of (pips_i / total_pips)^2 for each resource type i

An HHI of 1.0 means all pips come from one resource type. An HHI of 0.20 means pips are perfectly equal across all five types. Values below 0.30 indicate healthy diversity; values above 0.50 signal significant concentration risk.

Example 5.1 -- Computing the HHI A player's total pips by resource type: ore = 10, grain = 8, wood = 5, brick = 0, sheep = 3 (total = 26).

Fractions: ore = 10/26 = 0.385, grain = 8/26 = 0.308, wood = 5/26 = 0.192, brick = 0/26 = 0, sheep = 3/26 = 0.115.

HHI = 0.385^2 + 0.308^2 + 0.192^2 + 0^2 + 0.115^2 = 0.148 + 0.095 + 0.037 + 0 + 0.013 = 0.293.

This is borderline acceptable. The distribution is reasonably diverse across four resource types, but the complete absence of brick is a meaningful structural weakness. Any road or settlement building requires trading at a premium rate.

When Concentration Wins

Despite the argument for diversity, concentrated positions are genuinely superior in one specific circumstance: when a matching 2:1 port is accessible. A player who produces ore at a combined rate of 14 pips (two strong ore hexes) and sits on the ore port can convert excess ore into any other resource at half the standard cost. The effective exchange rate of 2:1 transforms what would otherwise be crippling overproduction into near-universal purchasing power.

Balance Across the Game Arc

The value of resource diversity is not constant over the course of a game. In the opening and early midgame, diversity is most critical because you need multiple resource types simultaneously to build roads, settlements, and eventually cities. Acquiring any one resource through trade is expensive, so self-sufficiency matters most when the build queue is longest.

In the late game, strategic focus narrows. A player at 8 victory points who needs only a city and a development card can afford to specialize entirely in ore, grain, and sheep. The diversity premium shrinks when you no longer need a broad range of resources to win. Recognizing this transition and adjusting your trading priorities accordingly is a practical and underappreciated skill.

Chapter 6

The First Two Settlements: A Mathematical Approach

Every resource you will ever collect in a game of Catan flows from decisions made before you take a single turn. The initial placement phase, in which each player places two settlements and two roads in a snake-draft order, is the highest-leverage decision sequence in the game. This chapter develops a decision framework for both placements.

The Snake Draft and Turn Order

In a four-player game, placement order follows a snake draft: Player 1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 3, 2, 1. Player 1 has unrestricted first access to the board's best vertex but must then wait through six more placements before their second pick. Player 4 places twice in succession and can specifically complement their first settlement without interference. Players 2 and 3 face the tightest constraints on their second placements, as the board is more crowded without the consecutive-pick advantage Player 4 enjoys.

In practice, going first is generally considered the strongest position because the single best vertex on any board is almost never available after three other players have picked. Going fourth is the second-strongest position because of the consecutive placement advantage. Going second or third, while perfectly viable, typically requires the sharpest positional reading of the available options.

First Placement Criteria

For the first settlement, the dominant criterion is absolute production score. At this stage of the game you know nothing about what resources your second settlement will provide, so the best available vertex in raw pip terms -- subject to a minimum resource-diversity floor of at least two distinct resource types -- is almost always correct. Aiming for at least 11 pips on the first settlement is a reasonable target. Accepting fewer than 9 is, in most cases, a mistake with lasting consequences.

Road direction is the secondary consideration. Think one step forward: given the board geometry, which direction from this vertex has the most remaining high-quality settlement locations? A first settlement that is already hemmed in on three sides by other players' intended paths has less long-term value than its pip count alone suggests.

The Second Placement

The second settlement must complement the first. The guiding principle is resource complementarity: the second settlement should provide the resource types your first settlement lacks, at a production score that keeps your combined total competitive.

The Starting Hand

Each second settlement produces an immediate bonus: one resource card from each adjacent hex. This starting hand of two or three cards determines what you can build on Turn 1. Most players aim for a starting hand that enables immediate road construction (1 wood + 1 brick), securing an expansion corridor before the board fills. A second settlement that yields only ore, sheep, and grain on all three adjacent tiles is strong for the long game but generates nothing spendable immediately, creating a tempo disadvantage in competitive play against players who can build roads from Turn 1.

Example 6.1 -- A Textbook Opening Player scans the board. The strongest available vertex, junction A, touches a 6 (ore), an 8 (grain), and a 5 (sheep): 14 pips, covering ore, grain, and sheep. Player takes it.

For the second settlement, all remaining strong vertices offer wood or brick access. Player selects junction B: a 9 (wood), a 4 (brick), and a 3 (wheat): 9 pips, covering wood, brick, and wheat. Combined pip total: 14 + 9 = 23. Resource coverage: all five types. Starting hand from B: 1 wood + 1 brick + 1 wheat. First action: build a road toward an open coastal corridor.

This is a strong, orthodox start. The 14-pip anchor at A ensures robust long-term production; the 9-pip second settlement at B fills every resource gap and provides the wood and brick needed to act immediately.

Correcting for Late Placement

Players picking third or fourth in the second round face a more constrained choice set. When no placement fully satisfies the complementarity requirement, the priority ordering is: (1) fill the most critical missing resource type, (2) maximize pips within the remaining options, and (3) choose the location with the best expansion shadow (the set of quality vertices reachable within two or three roads). Accepting a slightly lower pip count in exchange for meaningfully better expansion access is often the correct call in a crowded midboard.

Chapter 7

City Timing and Return on Investment

A city costs 3 ore and 2 grain, upgrades an existing settlement, adds one victory point, and doubles that settlement's production from that point forward. It is the single highest-return investment in the game by most metrics -- but only when timed correctly. This chapter uses return-on-investment (ROI) analysis to determine when upgrading is correct and when the same resources are better spent elsewhere.

The City as a Capital Investment

Think of a city as a capital expenditure that generates an ongoing production dividend. The cost is fixed at 5 resources (3 ore + 2 grain). The return is one additional resource card each time the underlying hex's number rolls. The break-even question is therefore: how many turns until the additional production from the city recoups the 5-resource build cost?

The city doubles production at that settlement, adding exactly p resources per turn (where p is the settlement's combined production probability). The payback period in turns is:

Payback period (turns) = 5 / p = 5 / (Production Score / 36) = 180 / Production Score

City vs. New Settlement

The alternative to a city (5 resources, 1 net VP) is usually a new settlement (4 resources plus road costs, 1 VP, access to new production terrain). Which is the better VP investment?

When quality settlement locations are still available, expansion often wins in the first half of the game because it opens new terrain and new resource types. When the board is filled and remaining locations are low-pip, cities win because the upgrade cost is lower than the combined road and settlement cost, and because the high-pip base amplifies the return. A useful rule of thumb: cities become the better VP path once your next available settlement location drops below 8 pips.

General Rule Build cities preferentially when (a) the settlement being upgraded has 10 or more pips, (b) the game is past the midpoint so fewer productive turns remain to recoup a new settlement's investment, and (c) strong new settlement locations are unavailable. Build new settlements when (a) quality locations remain, (b) you need resource types your current economy cannot produce, and (c) you have the wood and brick to expand roads without depleting ore and grain reserves.

The City Rush Strategy

Pursuing 5 cities as the primary path to 10 victory points requires 15 ore and 10 grain in total. Against a game with roughly 54 productive turns, you need to generate about 25 resources of combined ore and grain (accounting for other necessary builds) at a joint production rate of at least (25/54) x 36 = 16.7 combined pips across your ore and grain hexes. That requires roughly two high-value tokens on those resources -- a strong 8 on ore and a strong 6 on grain, for instance.

City rush is powerful precisely because it concentrates production into two hexes and one resource pair, enabling a fast and predictable VP path. It is vulnerable because both key hexes become high-value robber targets, and because any trade embargo on ore or grain by attentive opponents can stall the strategy entirely.

Part III

Resource Flow and Economic Efficiency

How resources move through your economy, where they get stuck, and how to eliminate waste at every stage.

Chapter 8

Production Rates and Bottlenecks

Chapters 4 through 7 focused on the per-settlement level: how good is this vertex, when should I upgrade it? Part III zooms out to the whole economy. A complete production picture requires treating all of your settlements and cities together as a single system and asking where the flow of resources is constrained. That question is the domain of bottleneck analysis.

Modeling Your Production as a Flow

Define your production rate for resource type R as the sum of pip values across all hexes producing R, divided by 36, multiplied by the appropriate settlement or city factor (1 for settlements, 2 for cities):

Rate(R) = [ sum of (pips_i x multiplier_i) for all hexes i producing R ] / 36

This gives you expected resources per turn for each type. Your full economy can then be expressed as a five-element production vector. For example: [wood: 0.194, brick: 0.167, ore: 0.278, grain: 0.222, sheep: 0.111]. Reading this vector tells you immediately where production is strong and where you are likely to experience chronic shortages.

Identifying the Bottleneck

A bottleneck is the resource whose production rate is lowest relative to the rate at which your strategy consumes it. The concept comes from manufacturing theory, where a production line's maximum output is always constrained by its slowest stage. The same logic applies to Catan economies.

The decision rule this implies: if you are holding 7 or more cards and can spend resources on any productive build -- even a slightly suboptimal one -- the expected cost of the forced discard usually exceeds the inefficiency of the suboptimal spend. Example 8.1 -- Spend Now or Wait? You hold 7 cards and need one more sheep to complete a settlement. Option A: wait one turn, risking a 7 (16.7% chance of losing roughly 3 to 4 cards). Expected cost: 0.167 x 3.5 = 0.58 resources. Option B: trade 2 grain for 1 sheep at a 2:1 grain port right now, costing 1 extra resource but eliminating the discard risk entirely.

At 8 cards, Option B is almost always superior. The 1-resource trade premium is smaller than the 0.58-resource expected discard cost -- and the discard cost grows with each subsequent turn you continue to hold. The mathematics strongly favor spending to stay below the discard threshold.
Chapter 9

Ports and Exchange Rates: Trading vs. Arbitrage

Trading with other players is Catan's most visible social mechanic, but it is also an economic one. Every trade has a rate, and every rate can be compared to a benchmark. Ports provide that benchmark: a fixed price list available to any player who has settled the right coastal vertex. This chapter analyzes port value quantitatively and introduces the concept of arbitrage within the Catan economy.

Port Types and Exchange Rates

The standard board contains nine port locations: five specialty 2:1 ports (one for each resource type) and four generic 3:1 ports. Without port access, the bank's default rate is 4:1. The efficiency gains from port access are substantial:

Quantifying Port Value

The value of a 2:1 specialty port depends directly on how much of the matching resource you produce. If you produce ore at a rate of 10 pips (0.278 resources per turn) and use the ore port to acquire grain, you receive effective grain at a rate of 0.139 resources per turn -- equivalent to the output of a 5-pip grain hex -- from conversion alone. The ore port is worth approximately 5 virtual grain pips per turn in that configuration.

More generally, a 2:1 port for resource R, given your production rate r of R, provides:

Effective acquired resource rate = r / 2 resources per turn

This is the contribution of the port beyond what your existing production already provides. For a player with r = 0.25 (9 pips of a matching resource), a 2:1 port generates 0.125 effective resources of any other type per turn -- roughly equivalent to a 4.5-pip hex. That is a significant ongoing benefit for a one-time settlement investment.

The Road to a Port: Is It Worth Building?

Settling on a port vertex requires either placing an initial settlement there or building roads to reach it during the game. A two-road investment (2 wood + 2 brick = 4 resources) to access a 2:1 port is efficient if the port will be used more than a handful of times. Using the valuation above:

Breakeven uses = Road cost / savings per use = 4 / 1 = 4 conversions

If your production rate is r = 0.25 of the matching resource per turn and you convert all surplus (say, half of production), you perform approximately 0.125 conversions per turn. The 4-resource road investment pays back in about 32 turns of production -- which is within the range of a typical game. If you will use the port more frequently -- because you overproduce the matching resource significantly -- payback is faster and the investment is more clearly worthwhile.

Arbitrage: Exploiting Price Differences

In financial markets, arbitrage is the practice of simultaneously exploiting price discrepancies across markets to earn a risk-free profit. In Catan, a constrained form of arbitrage exists when player-to-player trade rates diverge from port rates.

Consider: a desperate opponent offers you 2 grain for 1 ore. You hold a 2:1 grain port. Your grain costs you 2 grain to convert into 1 of anything -- but in this deal, you receive 2 grain for 1 ore and can immediately convert that grain at the port into 1 additional resource. The effective rate of your deal is 1 ore for 1 ore plus 1 of anything -- a better-than-even outcome.

More precisely: whenever an opponent's offer implies a resource valuation different from your port's implicit price, there is an arbitrage opportunity. A player who recognizes these discrepancies and structures trades to exploit them extracts real value that does not appear in their production statistics.

Practical Framing You do not need to run numbers in real time to apply this thinking. The simpler version: identify which resource is currently most scarce at the table (other players' desperation reveals this through their offers), and trade to supply exactly that resource in exchange for whatever you most need. Port access means your supply of the matching resource is effectively unlimited relative to others -- price accordingly.

Port Locking: A Strategic Denial

Each port's two coastal settlement vertices are a shared resource. The first player to settle one of them claims port access; if they later settle the second vertex of the same port, they lock all opponents out of that port permanently. Port locking is a form of territorial control with a calculable value: the denied opponent must pay 3:1 or 4:1 for everything the locked resource could have provided at 2:1. Over a game with 10 conversions, the efficiency loss to the locked opponent is approximately 10 additional resources -- a material disadvantage. Port locking is most worth pursuing when the port matches a resource you already produce abundantly and when a second settlement there provides acceptable production score in its own right.

Chapter 10

Development Cards: Probability and Expected Value

Development cards are the one significant element of Catan's economy that operates outside the dice-and-hex production framework. They cost a fixed mix of resources to acquire, they arrive with unknown contents drawn from a shuffled deck, and they provide effects ranging from mild (a single Knight) to game-altering (a Monopoly played on a well-stocked opponent). Understanding the deck's composition and how draw probabilities shift over the course of a game gives a material informational edge.

Draw Probabilities as Cards Leave the Deck

When you buy a development card early in the game, the deck is essentially full and draw probabilities match the composition percentages above. As cards are purchased throughout the game, the remaining deck composition shifts -- and tracking that shift gives you meaningful information about what you (or opponents) are likely to draw.

The relevant probability model is the hypergeometric distribution, which describes drawing without replacement from a finite population. If k cards of a certain type remain in a deck of size N, the probability of drawing one on your next purchase is simply k/N.

Example 10.1 -- Late-Game Draw Probabilities It is midgame. Ten development cards have been purchased by all players. Of those: eight were Knights (all played openly), one was a Road Building card (played), and one was a Victory Point card (now showing). The remaining deck contains 25 - 10 = 15 cards.

Remaining by type: Knights = 14 - 8 = 6. Victory Points = 5 - 1 = 4. Road Building = 2 - 1 = 1. Year of Plenty = 2. Monopoly = 2.

P(draw Knight) = 6/15 = 40.0%. P(draw Victory Point) = 4/15 = 26.7%. P(draw action card) = 5/15 = 33.3%.

The VP probability has risen from its baseline of 20% to nearly 27%. If you need one more VP to win and are buying development cards as your path there, this is a meaningfully favorable situation. Track the deck.

Expected Value of a Development Card Purchase

A development card costs 1 ore + 1 grain + 1 sheep: 3 resources in total. The expected value of each purchase depends on deck state, but in the early game from a full deck, a rough calculation runs as follows:

A Knight is worth approximately 1.0 to 1.5 resource-equivalents in direct impact (robber repositioning plus theft), plus a fractional share of the Largest Army bonus (worth 2 VP, or about 10 resource-equivalents of VP value). A VP card is worth 1 VP, which at a cost-per-VP rate of roughly 5 resources implies a value of 5 resource-equivalents. Action cards (Road Building, Year of Plenty, Monopoly) typically provide 2 to 5 resource-equivalents of direct benefit. Weighting by probability:

EV (full deck) = 0.56 x ~2.0 + 0.20 x ~5.0 + 0.24 x ~3.5 = 1.12 + 1.00 + 0.84 = ~3.0 resource-equivalents

Against a cost of 3 resources, this suggests development cards are approximately break-even in isolation -- which understates their value, because VP cards and Largest Army remain hidden until the winning moment, and because action cards often produce above their average impact against a specific opponent situation. Development cards are generally efficient purchases; the question is always whether the 3-resource cost could build something more urgent right now.

Largest Army: The Most Efficient VP Path

Largest Army awards 2 bonus VP to the first player to play at least 3 Knights, maintained as long as they hold the record. Since Knights represent 56% of the deck, the race for Largest Army begins implicitly with the first development card purchase.

The minimum investment to claim Largest Army from scratch: 3 Knights, requiring at least 3 development card purchases. At 3 resources per card, that is 9 resources for 2 VP -- a rate of 4.5 resources per VP. Compare: cities cost 5 resources per 1 net VP (5.0 resources per VP), and settlements plus road costs run 6 to 8 resources per VP. Largest Army is the most resource-efficient VP source in the game when achieved at minimum cost, which is why competitors who purchase development cards aggressively and track the Knight count are consistently difficult to contain.

The Hidden VP Asymmetry Victory Point cards are secret until the game ends. A player at 7 visible VPs who holds 3 hidden VP cards is actually at 10 and can declare victory immediately. Other players, unable to see this, may be spending resources suppressing a player at 8 or 9 visible VPs while the quiet accumulator of VP cards wins on their next turn. Tracking how many development cards each player has purchased -- not just how many they have played -- gives the best available signal of hidden VP accumulation. Five purchased but unplayed cards in one player's hand should raise attention regardless of their visible score.
